What is growing here?

Local rules, visible consequences

Every carrier uses the same rule: sample nearby trails and food, turn, move, deposit, consume. A persistent gel field forms where traffic accumulates. Excitation spreads between neighbouring patches of gel; recovery makes a recently excited patch harder to excite again.

The geometry is grown from a seeded random population. There are no drawn connections, named scout professions, target-seeking routes, or prerecorded signal paths. The colours show real simulation fields; lighting is a rendering effect.

Try it: let a network grow, freeze its structure, turn off spontaneous launches, and poke a route. Then unfreeze it and cut a path. Use Dam for a lasting barrier, and Open to remove it. Feed changes the local nutrient supply.

The shape test freezes two copies and resets their fast state. It compares propagation through the grown material with a spatial shuffle that preserves its values and the source neighbourhood. It reports what happened, including weak or reversed differences.

An artificial medium, with a neuroscience question

This is an excitable, adaptive material, not a validated neuron or a living organism. Launch sites arise from the same local charging rule everywhere. The rule is supplied; the locations and subsequent paths are not. Carriers do not reproduce or evolve, and food is replenished by an external reservoir.

The useful question is whether history changes the material enough to change future signal routing. Biological neurons require membrane biophysics, ion channels, actual morphology, and validation that this model does not have.

Trail formation follows the tradition of Jeff Jones’s Physarum models. The timing questions come from Drebitz et al. and Norman-Haignere et al.; the neural inspiration from Leterrier and Aizenbud et al.
